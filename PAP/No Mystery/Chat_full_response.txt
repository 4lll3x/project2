Yeah, I think there’s **a genuinely strong game concept here**, especially for a school project. The important thing is that you already have the beginnings of a _gameplay loop_, not just a story.

 What I'm hearing is roughly:

 > **Ex-police investigator → notices impossible pattern → superiors force him to stop → leaves police → investigates independently → individual locations/cases slowly reveal that the pattern is real → eventually discovers why the police wanted it buried.**

 And the really interesting part is that **the player isn't initially investigating “the paranormal.”** They're investigating ordinary cases that progressively become harder to explain.

 That gives you a good way to handle the horror without immediately going _"there are demons!"_.

 ## The part I particularly like

 The **car/van as a mobile base** is actually a really good mechanic.

 Instead of:

 > Find item → game magically gives you item → use item

 you can have:

 > Find problem → realize you _might_ have something useful in the van → decide whether going back is worth the time → return → bring equipment → try again.

 That makes the investigation feel more physical.

 For example:

 You enter an abandoned house.

 You discover a locked basement door.

 You don't have a lockpick.

 You remember there might be one in the van.

 You can:

 - Go back to the van and get it.
- Search the house for another way in.
- Find a code somewhere.
- Break something to create another entrance.
- Potentially discover something completely different while looking for another solution.

 And because returning to the van costs time, **the player has a choice rather than simply being told what to do.**

 That's a very nice system for an investigation game.

 # I'd make the investigation the actual gameplay

 One thing I'd be careful about is not making the game:

 > Walk around → click glowing clue → cutscene → next room.

 Your idea about having to actually manipulate the environment is much more interesting.

 For example:

 **You find a photograph.**

 The obvious information:

 > House photographed in 1998.

 But the player notices something strange in the background.

 Maybe there's a symbol.

 They photograph it.

 Later they find the same symbol in another case.

 Then they discover that the symbol is actually partially hidden underneath paint.

 Then they find a notebook containing three numbers.

 Those numbers correspond to:

 > 17 / 04 / 93

 The player has already encountered 17 and 04 somewhere else, but doesn't know what 93 means.

 Eventually they realize they're dates.

 And suddenly:

 **three apparently unrelated cases happened on the same date.**

 That's when the player starts thinking:

 > "Wait..."

 And importantly, **the protagonist doesn't necessarily know either.**

 That makes the player feel like an investigator rather than someone following quest markers.

 # Your "evaluation" idea could be really good

 I'd avoid making it something like:

 > Investigation Score: 87%

 because that makes it feel gamey.

 Instead, imagine each investigation ending with a **case report**.

 Something like:

 ### CASE 01 — REDWOOD HOUSE

 **Evidence recovered**

 - Photograph
- Police report
- Handwritten note
- Basement symbol

 **Possible explanations**

 - Trespassing
- Ritual activity
- Connection to Case #183

 **Unresolved**

 - Identity of person in photograph
- Meaning of symbol

 **Investigator's conclusion**

 > "The official report states that the house was abandoned after a burglary. Evidence recovered on site contradicts this."

 Then maybe the game gives you a subtle assessment:

 **Investigation complete.**

 And the next mission changes depending on what you found.

 You don't necessarily need branching storylines. You can have **optional evidence** that changes what the player knows.

 That's much easier to develop.

 # I wouldn't make the player "solve" the connection manually

 You actually identified a problem yourself:

 > "the player do it put that doesnt make sense and its hard"

 Exactly.

 You don't want the player to have to personally determine:

 > Case A + Case B + Case C = conspiracy

 because someone can just look up the solution.

 Instead, let the **character's journal gradually organize discovered information**.

 For example:

```
CASE #014
└── Missing person
    ├── Location: Blackwood
    ├── Date: 17/04/1993
    └── Symbol: ◇

CASE #031
└── House fire
    ├── Location: Red Valley
    ├── Date: 17/04/1993
    └── Symbol: ◇

CASE #???
└── [Unknown]
    └── ...
```

 When the player discovers the symbol, the game can automatically add:

 > **Possible connection detected**

 But **the player still had to discover it.**

 That's the sweet spot.

 # And I'd make the "No Mystery" thing important

 Honestly, **"No Mystery" is a cool phrase for this game.**

 It could become an actual recurring thing.

 The first time:

 > **CASE CLOSED — NO MYSTERY**

 The player doesn't think much of it.

 Then another case:

 > **NO MYSTERY**

 Then another.

 Eventually the protagonist notices that the same wording appears in completely unrelated police reports.

 And then:

 > **NO MYSTERY**

 isn't just a phrase anymore.

 It's evidence.

 Maybe it's an internal classification.

 Maybe police officers were instructed to use that wording.

 Maybe it's connected to the people suppressing the investigations.

 Maybe it's something even stranger.

 That gives you a mystery **inside the mystery**.

 And you don't have to decide immediately whether it's a cult, government organization, supernatural entity, etc.

 I'd actually recommend **not deciding the exact answer too early.**

 Figure out what the player should _feel_ first:

 > "Someone doesn't want me looking at these cases."

 Then later:

 > "Someone has been manipulating these cases."

 Then:

 > "Why?"

 Then:

 > "Holy shit, what actually happened?"

 # The cult idea

 A cult could work, but I'd resist making it the obvious explanation.

 You could deliberately make the player think:

 **Cult.**

 Then find:

 - Ritual documents
- Symbols
- Strange recordings
- Bodies
- Missing persons
- Religious objects
- Repeated dates

 Everything screams cult.

 But then you discover police documents about the same thing from **40 years ago**.

 And then:

 > The "cult" isn't actually the origin.

 Or perhaps the cult itself doesn't understand what it's worshipping.

 That lets you transition from **detective mystery → conspiracy → paranormal horror**.

 Which fits your original idea extremely well.

 # The van inventory is probably your most interesting gameplay system

 I'd keep it relatively simple because this is a school project.

 Instead of simulating weight realistically, have something like:

 ### Player

 **Pockets**

 - 5 slots

 ### Bag

 **6–8 slots**

 ### Van

 **Large storage**

 - 20+ slots

 Then items have categories:

 **Investigation**

 - Camera
- Flashlight
- Evidence bags
- Notebook
- UV light
- Tape measure

 **Tools**

 - Lockpick
- Screwdriver
- Pliers
- Crowbar

 **Electronics**

 - Laptop
- Signal detector
- Phone
- Recorder

 And then some objects are **large**:

 > Crowbar — 2 slots

 > Camera — 2 slots

 > Toolbox — 4 slots

 That gives you inventory decisions without requiring an insane inventory system.

 # Don't make everything a "key item"

 This is important.

 If the player sees:

 > 🔑 **BASEMENT KEY**

 they immediately know:

 > "Ah, basement."

 Instead:

 **Rusty key**

 And the player has to remember:

 > "Wait, didn't I see a locked cabinet upstairs?"

 That feels much more like investigation.

 Likewise:

 > **Old photograph**

 rather than:

 > **PHOTOGRAPH — REQUIRED FOR CASE CONNECTION**

 # The computer in the van is a great idea too

 But again, **keep its scope under control**.

 You don't actually need to create Google.

 You can make an in-game fake search engine with a predefined database.

 The player searches:

 > `Blackwood 1993`

 and gets:

```
BLACKWOOD — LOCAL ARCHIVE

17 April 1993
Residential fire reported...

18 April 1993
Police investigation opened...

[ARTICLE UNAVAILABLE]
```

 Then later they discover:

 > `Blackwood 1993 17 April`

 and something new appears.

 That creates the illusion of a huge information system without you needing to build one.

 Same thing with police databases.

 # About 2D vs 2.5D vs 3D

 For a **12th-grade computer-science school project**, I would strongly consider **2D or 2.5D**.

 Not because 3D is impossible, but because 3D explodes your workload.

 With 3D you suddenly need:

 - Models
- Textures
- Animations
- Lighting
- Character controller
- Camera
- Collision
- Environment modeling
- Doors
- Physics
- UI
- etc.

 Whereas a 2D game can put much more of your effort into the thing that actually makes your game unique:

 **investigation design.**

 And honestly, a deliberately old-looking 2D/2.5D aesthetic could fit this game extremely well.

 You could make it look:

 - dark
- grainy
- low-resolution
- slightly distorted
- limited color palette
- old surveillance-camera aesthetic
- VHS-like effects

 You don't need photorealism.

 In fact, **not showing everything clearly can make the horror better.**

 # Games I'd look at

 Since you mentioned **Enigma of Fear** and **Hellsign**, there are some interesting references.

 - Enigma of Fear  — probably the closest reference for your idea mechanically. Investigation, environments, clues, supernatural mystery, documents, puzzles, and 2D/3D presentation.
- HellSign  — particularly useful for the idea of investigating locations, gathering evidence and dealing with supernatural threats.
- The Chillas Art games  — useful reference for smaller-scale locations and environmental horror. A lot of their games show how you can create tension without gigantic environments.
- SIGNALIS  — worth studying for atmosphere, environmental storytelling, inventory limitations, documents and the way information is delivered.
- Stories Untold  — **very relevant to your computer/terminal idea.** It uses different interfaces and investigation-like interaction to create mystery.
- Fears to Fathom  — useful for studying relatively contained locations and how mundane environments can become threatening.
- Return of the Obra Dinn  — not horror in the same way, but **extremely useful for studying investigation as gameplay**. The player has to reconstruct what happened from evidence rather than being spoon-fed the answer.
- Her Story  — great reference for the idea of searching through fragmented information and gradually understanding a larger mystery.
- The Painscreek Killings  — another particularly useful reference for environmental investigation without constantly holding the player's hand.

 I think **Enigma of Fear \+ HellSign + Return of the Obra Dinn \+ Stories Untold** is a particularly interesting combination to study for your project.

 ## One thing I'd change from your current idea

 Don't make the goal of every level:

 > **Find the thing that connects this level to the next level.**

 That risks making the game feel like:

 > "Find clue → unlock next mission."

 Instead, make the goal:

 > **Understand what happened here.**

 The connection to the next case should be a **consequence of investigating correctly.**

 So the player enters a house thinking:

 > "I'm investigating a disappearance."

 They leave thinking:

 > "This disappearance wasn't what the police said it was."

 And _then_ they notice:

 > "Wait. This symbol appeared in Case #27."

 **That's much more satisfying.**

 And honestly, before you start making anything, I'd want to figure out **three things with you**:

 1. **What exactly is the underlying mystery?** — cult, entity, conspiracy, experiment, something else, or intentionally ambiguous.
2. **What does one complete investigation actually look like moment-to-moment?** — because that's the core gameplay loop.
3. **How big this needs to be for the school project** — because we can design a version that's actually finishable rather than accidentally creating a 20-hour horror game as a student project. 😭

 If you want, we can basically **design the first investigation together from beginning to end**—location, clues, inventory, puzzles, van mechanics, journal, what the player can miss, the final reveal, and what connects it to Investigation #2. That would probably tell you very quickly whether the whole concept actually works as a game.
